Restore the room,
let the garden in.

Architectural, interior and landscape design for the outreach store of the Francisco Peláez R. Ethnobotanical Garden in Puebla. Instead of building something new, the project restores the brick room that was already there: same walls, same windows, same entrance doors, finished with lime, reclaimed timber and living plants, and opened onto an ethnobotanical forecourt garden.

01

The Context

A garden of more than 1,200 herbs and flowers needed a room to meet its visitors.

The Ethnobotanical Garden cultivates plants from all over the world and shares Puebla's botanical and cultural heritage through environmental education. Its outreach store is where that mission meets the public: plants, seeds, natural products and bird food, sold in a space that should feel like an extension of the garden itself.

The brief asked for three things: make products easy to see, give staff a productive place to work, and do it all within the client's most critical constraint, a low budget.

The garden already owned a small single-storey brick building with a concrete roof slab, a pair of timber entrance doors and windows on both long sides. The first design decision was not to replace it. The building is sound, it belongs to the place, and everything it already holds (its bricks, its slab, its doors, its daylight) is material that does not have to be made, transported or thrown away again.

02

Design Concept

The most sustainable building is the one that is already standing.

Life-cycle studies that compare keeping and refurbishing a building against demolishing it and building again find that refurbishment carries less embodied energy and lower environmental impacts[C1, C2]. GreenStore applies that idea at the scale of one small room. Every decision follows the same order: keep what is there, repair what is worn, reuse what can be salvaged, and add only what is missing, in a way that can be taken away again.

01 · Keep

The shell stays.

Brick walls, piers, concrete floor and roof slab, the window openings and the entrance all stay where they are. No demolition, no new foundations, no new openings cut into the walls.

02 · Repair

Mend, don't replace.

The original timber doors are repaired and oil-finished in place. Window frames are eased and resealed, broken panes replaced with safety glass, brick joints repointed in lime.

03 · Reuse

Second-life materials.

Shelving, island and counter are built from reclaimed pine. Sound bricks recovered during the repairs edge the garden beds; clean rubble becomes sub-base for the walk.

04 · Add, lightly

Only what is missing.

A planted roof, a living wall, lime finishes and a forecourt garden. Each addition is screwed, laid or planted, never cast in, so the room can always return to what it was.

03

Design Principles

Three ideas guided every decision, from the floor plan to the joinery of the counter.

Sustainability

Reuse first: the existing building, its doors and windows are kept and repaired. What is added is reclaimed, local or living: reclaimed-pine furniture, lime and terracotta, a living wall, and a planted roof that sends its water to a cistern and a rain garden.

Aesthetic

A warm palette of terracotta floor, lime-white walls and natural timber. Soft cove lighting and a woven pendant let the plants and products carry the color.

Minimalism

Every dimension sits on a 15 cm module, every piece of furniture on a 60 cm grid, and one clear axis runs from the garden through the existing doorway to the counter. Nothing competes with the product.

Interior Design

Warm, hand-made, daylit and alive.

The interior palette starts from what the building already offers: the red of its brick, the light from its windows, the grain of its timber doors. Everything added is chosen to sit quietly beside those originals, so the room reads as one restored whole rather than a new shop fitted into an old box.

The Entrance

The original doors stay. They are repaired, not replaced.

Opening

Unchanged

same piers, same lintel

Doors D01

Repaired

existing pair, 2.10 × 2.40 m

The entrance is part of the building's character, and it already works: a pair of glazed timber leaves on the main axis, wide enough to open the room to the garden. So it stays exactly as it was. The leaves are taken down, their joints reglued, broken panes replaced with laminated safety glass, worn hinges and handles renewed, and the timber oiled, then hung back in the same frame.

Keeping the opening means no wall is cut, no lintel is replaced and no brick is wasted. With both leaves open the pair gives about 94.5 cm clear through each leaf, and a 1.50 m turning circle stays free just inside.

Dimensions are design dimensions set to the module; verify on site.

Functional Diagram

How the spaces relate.

Every space connects back to the feature island, so a visitor never reaches a dead end. The two display walls, one for distillates and one for infusions, both lead to the counter, where sales and staff work meet.

Visual links keep the garden present: it is seen through the windows from both displays, and the living wall is always in view from the counter. This relationship diagram is the one drawing in the set that is not to scale.

Building Systems

Lighting, power, irrigation and drainage on the same axes and module, ready for the engineers who will size them. Cables run in the timber cove and surface conduit, so the existing brick is never chased.

09

Landscape Design

The store's axis walks out into a forecourt planted with the useful plants of Puebla.

The garden around the store is laid out on the building's own axes and modules. The main axis runs out of the existing doorway as a 1.80 m walk; paths and beds are 1.20 m multiples; the plaza paving keeps the 60 cm fit-out grid. The forecourt band is 12.00 by 15.75 m.

The landscape follows the same rule as the building: work with the ground that is there. The existing soil is kept and improved rather than removed, cut and fill stay on site, and the beds are rain-fed so that, once established, they live on the seasons and on the roof water.

Puebla sits at about 2,140 m with a temperate subhumid climate: summer rains, about six humid months, 400 to 800 mm a year, a small temperature range and occasional winter frost[1, 2]. The palette leans on rain-fed annuals for the wet season and drought-tolerant perennials for the dry one, and the roof water stays on site.

Landscape Strategy

A garden that reuses its ground, its water and its own plants.

Ground

Keep the soil.

Existing topsoil is kept, loosened and fed with the garden's own compost. Levels are balanced on site, so no soil is trucked in or out.

Materials

Salvage first.

Bricks recovered from the repairs edge the beds; crushed rubble is the sub-base of the walk. Basalt pavers are laid on sand, not concrete, so they can be lifted and the rain soaks through.

Water

Every drop stays.

The green roof slows the rain, the downspouts fill a 1,100 L cistern for the living wall, and the overflow sinks into the rain garden instead of the street.

Plants

From the garden itself.

Useful native plants of Puebla, propagated where possible from the Ethnobotanical Garden's own collection, rain-fed annuals for the wet season and drought-tolerant perennials for the dry one.

Maize, beans and squash

Bed MI · 12.2 m²

The three-crop plot that anchors the diet of the Puebla countryside, with the wild greens that grow among it: amaranth greens, Aztec broccoli and Bolivian coriander. The three crops root at different depths, so together they draw more from the soil.[12, 13, 10]

Medicinal and aromatic

Bed ME · 12.2 m²

Sweet-scented marigold and giant hyssop for teas, wormseed and oregano lippia for the kitchen, and Aztec marigold for the Day of the Dead. Each bed carries a label the store can extend into its products.[14, 15, 16, 17]

Dry garden, Tehuacán Valley

Bed XE · 27.9 m²

The Tehuacán-Cuicatlán Valley in Puebla's arid south has 1,608 recorded useful plant species. Agaves, sour pitaya and bilberry cacti and the endemic slender ponytail palm fill the hot west side.[3, 4, 6, 7, 8]

Dye and ritual

Bed TI · 27.9 m²

Anil indigo for blue, prickly pear hosting the cochineal insect for red, and marigold for orange: the colours of the region's textiles, grown beside the store that sells natural products.[22, 23, 24, 17]

Rain garden

Bed RG · 14.4 m²

Behind the store, a planted basin takes the roof water. The rear downspouts fill a 1,100 L cistern first, which supplies the living-wall drip line, and the overflow soaks in here.[25]

Furniture Design

A modular desk that sells on one side and works on the other.

The front face displays jars, bottles and seed packets in open cubbies. The back holds a computer station and storage boxes for stock. Three identical D-600 modules, built from reclaimed timber panels and screwed rather than glued, can be assembled, repaired, extended or taken apart without specialist labor. None of it is fixed to the old walls.
